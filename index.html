No Envy

Split the rent so nobody wants to swap rooms.

The rooms are not equal, so an even split is not fair. Each housemate says what each room is worth to them. You get who lives where and what each pays, and a table to check that nobody would rather have another room at its rent.

2 What each room is worth to each housemate

Each housemate shares out the whole rent across the rooms: more on the rooms they like, less on the others. The amounts add up to the rent because the rent has to be paid in full. The same amount on two rooms means you would be equally happy with either. Honest numbers are the safe choice: you never pay more than your own number for the room you get (give or take 1 from rounding).

How it works

  1. Rooms go where they are valued most. Out of every way to hand out the rooms, the one with the highest total of everyone's own numbers is used. Any split where nobody wants to swap has to start there.
  2. Rents are set so nobody wants to swap. For each person, their own number for their room minus its rent is at least as good as that for any other room.
  3. Among all such rents, the ones kindest to the worst-off person are picked. There is usually a range of rents that work. This picks the ones that leave the least-well-off person as well off as possible, then the next, and so on.

The method follows the maximin envy-free rent division of Gal, Mash, Procaccia and Zick (2016); the “then the next, and so on” of step 3 is added so that there is one answer. The arithmetic runs in your browser, in plain code you can read. It is a calculation, not legal or financial advice.

Two honest limits. Rents are whole numbers, so now and then someone (sometimes more than one person) ends up a unit short of a perfect match (one unit at most in every sheet we have tested); the result says so, and by how much, when it happens. And someone who misstates their numbers on purpose can sometimes come out ahead, as with any such method.

For other sites and agents

Lease wording. No Envy splits rent; it does not read leases. For what a clause in a rental agreement means in plain words, there is Plainly, an independent site written for readers in India. The optional assistant can ask Plainly’s assistant for you: it first shows a card with the site and the start of the question, and nothing is contacted until you allow it. Then your browser fetches Plainly’s public agent files from its site and runs them here, with the AI provider you picked. The page passes along only the assistant’s question, never your rent sheet, and the assistant is told to send only the clause you asked about. Plainly’s answer is general information, not legal advice.

For other agent sites. The split itself is shared: another site's assistant can call this site's agent network (no_envy, next to this page's agent-manifest.json) and use its try_split action to share out any cost among people who value the parts differently. It only works on the numbers in the call. It cannot see a sheet typed on this page, and it stores nothing.
